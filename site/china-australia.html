Shipping from China to Australia: Complete Guide for Importers (2026)

Shipping from China to Australia can be straightforward when the shipment is planned before the cargo leaves the factory. The freight rate is only one part of the landed cost. Importers also need to consider the shipping method, Incoterm, container size, destination charges, customs clearance, GST, biosecurity, packaging and final delivery.

This guide explains the complete process for commercial importers—from supplier pickup in China to delivery in Australia—and shows where preventable logistics costs usually occur.

1. What is the best way to ship from China to Australia?

The best method depends on cargo volume, urgency, value, product restrictions and final delivery requirements. Most commercial shipments use one of the following options.

FCL — Full Container Load

FCL means you book the whole container. It is commonly used for furniture, cabinets, building materials, machinery, lighting, industrial products and larger mixed orders.

  • Better cost per cubic metre when cargo volume is high enough
  • Less handling than LCL because the cargo does not need to be consolidated with multiple importers
  • More control over loading, securing and supplier consolidation
  • Common equipment: 20GP and 40HQ

Learn more about ocean freight from China.

LCL — Less than Container Load

LCL allows several shippers to share container space. It can be a practical option for smaller commercial orders, first shipments and cargo collected from multiple suppliers.

Do not compare LCL only by the ocean freight amount. Origin handling, consolidation, destination unpacking and local charges can materially change the total cost.

Air freight

Air freight is suitable for urgent, higher-value or smaller cargo. Chargeable weight is usually based on the greater of actual weight and volumetric weight, so carton dimensions matter as much as kilograms.

For urgent cargo, see our air freight services.

Door-to-door shipping

Door-to-door service can combine supplier pickup, export handling, international transport, destination coordination and final delivery. The exact responsibility depends on the agreed Incoterm and service scope.

Review our door-to-door shipping options before deciding between DAP, DDP or another structure.

2. Major shipping routes from China to Australia

Common China departure ports include Shenzhen/Yantian, Shekou, Guangzhou/Nansha, Ningbo, Shanghai, Qingdao, Xiamen and Tianjin. The most efficient port usually depends on where your factories are located.

Major Australian container gateways include Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Fremantle and Adelaide. A cheaper ocean rate from a distant Chinese port may not be cheaper overall once domestic trucking and handling are added.

3. How long does shipping from China to Australia take?

Transit time depends on origin, destination, carrier, direct versus transshipment service, port congestion and schedule changes. For planning purposes, many ocean shipments fall within roughly a 2–5 week port-to-port range. Fremantle or transshipment services can take longer than some direct East Coast services.

The vessel transit shown on a schedule is not the same as total door-to-door lead time. A realistic plan may also include factory pickup, cut-off time, export clearance, waiting for the sailing, Australian customs and biosecurity clearance, cargo release and final delivery.

Planning tip: ask the forwarder whether the quoted transit is port-to-port or door-to-door, and whether the service is direct or transshipped.

4. How much does shipping from China to Australia cost?

There is no permanent fixed rate. Ocean freight changes with vessel capacity, shipping-line pricing, equipment availability, season, trade demand, port conditions and route adjustments. A quotation should therefore include a validity period.

A complete landed-cost discussion may include:

StagePossible cost items
China originFactory pickup, trucking, warehouse handling, loading, export declaration, documentation and local charges
International transportOcean or air freight plus applicable carrier surcharges
Australia destinationShipping-line/local charges, customs clearance, biosecurity inspection or treatment if required, terminal/depot charges and final delivery
Government chargesGST, customs duty where applicable and other statutory charges relevant to the goods

A low ocean freight number is not necessarily the lowest total shipment cost. Always compare the same scope of service.

5. 20GP vs 40HQ: which container should you choose?

A 20GP is often suitable for dense or heavy cargo that does not need the extra cubic capacity of a 40HQ. A 40HQ is widely used for bulky commercial goods such as furniture, cabinets, building materials, packaging products and mixed supplier cargo.

Do not choose a container based only on theoretical cubic capacity. Actual loading depends on carton dimensions, pallets, weight distribution, stacking restrictions, fragile cargo and internal container dimensions. A practical loading plan can save more than a small reduction in freight rate.

6. FCL vs LCL: which is more cost-effective?

There is no single CBM threshold that works for every route. LCL normally suits smaller shipments, while FCL becomes increasingly attractive as volume grows and LCL origin/destination handling accumulates.

  • Choose LCL when cargo volume is small and flexibility matters more than the lowest unit cost.
  • Compare both options when the shipment is getting close to a commercially meaningful container load.
  • Choose FCL when a full container provides better total cost, handling control or cargo protection.

7. Choosing the right Incoterm: EXW, FOB, CIF, DAP and DDP

Incoterms define important cost and responsibility boundaries. When comparing supplier offers, always ask: “What Incoterm is this quotation based on?”

TermPractical importer view
EXWThe buyer takes on substantial logistics responsibility from the supplier's premises, including origin pickup and export arrangements.
FOBCommon for container trade. The supplier handles agreed origin responsibilities up to shipment on board at the named port; the buyer arranges the main freight and destination process.
CIFThe seller pays freight and insurance to the named destination port, but who pays freight and when risk transfers are not the same issue. Destination costs can still remain with the buyer.
DAPThe seller arranges transportation to the named destination, while import clearance and import duties/taxes generally remain the buyer's responsibility.
DDPThe seller assumes extensive delivery and import responsibilities, subject to local legal and tax rules. It should not be treated as a simple “all included” label without checking the importer/compliance structure.

8. Australian customs clearance

Commercial imports need accurate descriptions, values and tariff classification. Common documents include a commercial invoice, packing list and bill of lading or air waybill. Depending on the product, additional permits, certificates, origin evidence or biosecurity documents may be required.

Tariff codes beyond the international six-digit HS level can differ between countries, so a supplier's Chinese tariff code should not automatically be treated as the final Australian classification. For uncertain cases, use an Australian licensed customs broker or seek formal classification advice.

9. Import duty, ChAFTA and GST

The China–Australia Free Trade Agreement (ChAFTA) eliminated Australian import tariffs on eligible Chinese-origin goods according to its tariff schedule, but preferential treatment still depends on correct classification, rules of origin and supporting documentation. Importers should confirm whether the specific goods qualify instead of assuming every product is automatically duty-free.

For taxable importations, Australian Border Force states that GST is generally 10% of the value of the taxable importation. That value can include the customs value, customs duty, international transport and insurance to Australia, and certain other applicable taxes.

Important: GST is not necessarily calculated as 10% of the supplier's factory invoice alone. Ask your customs broker to estimate the tax position before shipment if landed cost is important to the buying decision.

10. Australian biosecurity: check before the goods leave China

Australia applies strict biosecurity controls. Requirements vary by commodity, country of origin, packaging and use. The Australian Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry directs importers to the BICON system to check product-specific import conditions.

Biosecurity issues can involve soil, plant material, insects, animal material, seeds, timber, bamboo, used machinery contamination, food, agricultural products and natural materials.

If the goods do not meet import conditions, possible outcomes can include inspection, treatment, storage, re-export or destruction at the importer's expense. Preventing the problem in China is usually easier than solving it after arrival.

11. ISPM 15 and wooden packaging for Australia

Timber packaging deserves special attention. Australian biosecurity conditions cover pallets, crates, cases, bearers, skids, dunnage and other packaging materials. Solid timber packaging can comply through ISPM 15 or other accepted BICON treatment options, depending on the applicable conditions.

ISPM 15 compliant wood packaging must meet the standard's bark tolerance, approved treatment and marking requirements. Australia also requires packaging to be free from biosecurity risk material; an ISPM 15 mark does not protect packaging that becomes contaminated or reinfested after treatment.

Processed wood products such as plywood or particle board can be treated differently from raw solid timber, so identify the actual packaging material rather than simply writing “wooden case”.

Official reference: DAFF — ISPM 15 and Timber and bamboo packaging.

12. Destination charges in Australia

Destination charges are often misunderstood because different carriers, ports and shipment types use different fee structures. Depending on the shipment, charges may relate to shipping-line local fees, delivery order/EDO release, terminal or depot handling, documentation, customs brokerage and delivery.

Before booking, ask what is included at destination and what will be billed separately. A quotation that looks cheaper at origin can become more expensive if destination costs are higher.

13. EDO and container release

For an FCL import, vessel arrival does not automatically mean the container can be collected. Shipping-line charges, customs clearance, biosecurity status, document requirements and cargo release usually need to be completed before trucking can be arranged.

Where an Electronic Delivery Order (EDO) or equivalent release is required, obtain it early enough to avoid losing free time at the terminal or depot.

14. Demurrage, detention and port storage

These terms are sometimes used differently by carriers and terminals, but the commercial risk is the same: delays after arrival can become expensive.

  • Demurrage: commonly associated with keeping the carrier's container at the terminal beyond free time.
  • Detention / container hire: commonly associated with keeping the container outside the terminal too long before empty return.
  • Terminal or port storage: may be charged by the terminal when cargo or containers remain beyond applicable free periods.

Prepare documents, customs clearance, biosecurity checks, carrier payments and trucking before arrival whenever possible.

15. How to choose a freight forwarder from China to Australia

Do not choose only by the lowest freight rate. Ask whether the forwarder regularly handles your route and cargo type, which carriers they use, whether the service is direct or transshipped, what destination charges may apply, how free time works, and how they coordinate Australian customs and biosecurity issues.

One of the most useful questions is:

“What could cause problems with this shipment, and what should we do before shipping to prevent them?”

A good answer should discuss the cargo, packaging, documents, route and destination process—not only the ocean freight price.

16. Example shipping process from China to Australia

  1. Confirm supplier, product and Incoterm.
  2. Collect quantity, packing, dimensions, weight, CBM and cargo-ready date.
  3. Check Australian import, customs and biosecurity requirements.
  4. Compare FCL, LCL, air freight or door-to-door options.
  5. Book the carrier and confirm schedule/cut-off.
  6. Arrange supplier pickup, warehouse receiving or container loading.
  7. Complete China export declaration and shipping documents.
  8. Prepare Australian clearance before arrival where possible.
  9. Pay applicable carrier, customs and destination charges.
  10. Obtain cargo/container release and arrange final delivery.
  11. For FCL, return the empty container within the allowed period.

18. Frequently asked questions

How long does shipping from China to Australia take?

Ocean freight commonly takes several weeks depending on origin, destination, carrier, schedule and whether the service is direct or transshipped. Door-to-door lead time is longer because pickup, customs clearance and final delivery are also included.

How much does shipping from China to Australia cost?

There is no single fixed price. Cost depends on origin, destination, FCL or LCL, container type, cargo, carrier schedule, season, destination charges, customs clearance and final delivery. Current cargo details are required for an accurate quotation.

Is FCL cheaper than LCL from China to Australia?

LCL is usually suitable for smaller shipments. As cargo volume increases, FCL can become more cost-effective because LCL origin and destination handling charges accumulate with volume.

Can I ship goods on wooden pallets to Australia?

Yes, but timber packaging must meet Australian biosecurity import conditions. Solid timber packaging may need to comply with ISPM 15 or another accepted treatment option and must be correctly declared and free from biosecurity risk material.

How is GST calculated on imported goods in Australia?

For taxable importations, GST is generally 10% of the value of the taxable importation. The calculation can include customs value, customs duty, international transport and insurance to Australia, and certain other applicable taxes.

What documents are commonly needed to import from China to Australia?

Commercial imports commonly require a commercial invoice, packing list and transport document such as a bill of lading or air waybill. Depending on the goods, tariff classification, origin evidence, permits, certificates or biosecurity documents may also be required.

Official references for Australian importers

  • Australian Border Force — GST and other taxes when importing
  • DFAT — Guide to using ChAFTA to export or import
  • DAFF — BICON Biosecurity Import Conditions
  • DAFF — ISPM 15

This guide is general logistics information, not legal, tax or customs advice. Import conditions, carrier rules, government charges and freight rates can change. Product-specific requirements should be confirmed with the relevant Australian authority and, where appropriate, a licensed customs broker before shipment.
